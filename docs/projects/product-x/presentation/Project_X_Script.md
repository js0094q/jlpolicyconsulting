Presentation Script

1. Opening / Framing (30–45 seconds)

“Thank you for the time today. I’m going to walk through how Product X fits into the current post-EVT treatment paradigm, and more importantly, how it behaves under real-world hospital economics and reimbursement.

At a high level, Product X presents as a clinically differentiated therapy addressing a clear gap, recovery after reperfusion, but its adoption is not going to be determined by clinical value alone. It will be determined by how it fits within the DRG-based inpatient payment system, and whether that gap is bridged through NTAP and eventually DRG recalibration.  

So the goal of this discussion is to separate clinical attractiveness from financial viability, and show where those align and where they do not.”

⸻

2. Transition to Analysis Approach (How and Why)

“I approached this analysis from a hospital decision-maker perspective, not from a clinical or sponsor perspective.

That means three things guided the work:

First, everything is anchored to the DRG. EVT cases are reimbursed as a bundled payment, typically in the $25,000 to $50,000 range, so any incremental therapy is evaluated against a fixed revenue envelope.  

Second, I separated gross value vs. recoverable value. Clinical outcomes like reduced infarct volume or improved mRS are important, but hospitals translate those into operational metrics, length of stay, ICU days, and discharge disposition.

Third, I stress-tested the assumptions against real utilization constraints. For example, if a therapy needs 10+ days of LOS reduction to break even, but baseline LOS is 6–8 days, that is structurally impossible, regardless of clinical benefit.

So the analysis is intentionally grounded in:
	•	Real DRG constraints
	•	Real hospital cost structure
	•	Real adoption behavior under financial pressure

Not theoretical value.”

⸻

3. Core Insight (What the Analysis Shows)

“The key takeaway is that Product X is clinically compelling but economically constrained at launch.

On the clinical side:
	•	Strong efficacy and outcomes signal, particularly on functional independence and discharge to home
	•	Clear mechanism to reduce downstream cost by improving recovery
	•	No major safety barriers

On the operational side:
	•	The 48-hour infusion adds workload in the ICU setting, but does not fundamentally change site of care
	•	It increases intensity early, but may reduce total resource use over the full episode

On the economic side, which is where the real decision happens:

At launch:
	•	Product X is fully absorbed into the DRG
	•	That makes it a hospital cost center by default  

Even with:
	•	~3–5 day LOS reduction
	•	Improved discharge patterns

That only generates roughly $10K–$15K in recoverable value, which caps what hospitals can absorb without additional reimbursement.

So:
	•	Below ~$20K → absorbable, adoption possible
	•	~$25K–$35K → requires NTAP to be viable
	•	Above that → becomes reimbursement-dependent, not offset-driven  

That is the central constraint.”

⸻

4. NTAP Framing (Critical Point)

“NTAP is not optional in this scenario, it is the enabling mechanism.

Without NTAP:
	•	Hospitals take full financial risk
	•	Adoption is limited to early adopters and select patients

With NTAP:
	•	Up to ~65% of cost is covered
	•	A $30K therapy effectively becomes ~$10K net exposure
	•	That shifts the decision from ‘this is a loss’ to ‘this is manageable’  

That is why adoption moves from:
	•	~10–20% without NTAP
	•	To ~50–70% with NTAP
	•	To ~70–90% once DRGs adjust  

So the real story is not just clinical value, it is timing of reimbursement alignment.”

⸻

5. Close (Positioning Statement)

“The bottom line is:

Product X addresses a real and important clinical gap, recovery after EVT.

But hospitals will not adopt it based on clinical value alone. They will adopt it when:
	•	The net cost is manageable
	•	The reimbursement pathway is clear
	•	And the operational burden is justified by measurable outcomes

So success depends on:
	•	Securing NTAP early
	•	Demonstrating real-world outcomes that support DRG recalibration
	•	And aligning price with what the system can absorb at each stage

That is the path from promising therapy to standard of care.”

⸻

Anticipated Questions + Answers

1. “If the outcomes are that strong, why wouldn’t hospitals just adopt?”

“They will adopt selectively, not broadly.

The constraint is financial. Under DRGs, hospitals do not get paid more for better outcomes, they get paid the same. So unless the cost is offset or reimbursed, better outcomes alone do not justify adoption at scale.  ”

⸻

2. “Why isn’t LOS reduction enough to justify the cost?”

“Because the math does not work.

At $25K–$35K:
	•	You would need ~8–17 days of LOS reduction to fully offset
	•	Actual achievable reduction is ~3–5 days

So LOS contributes value, but cannot close the gap on its own.  ”

⸻

3. “Would payers restrict access?”

“Not at the bedside in the inpatient setting.

Coverage is generally not the barrier. The barrier is:
	•	Hospital willingness to absorb cost
	•	Internal P&T and value analysis decisions

At higher price points, payers may reinforce documentation and appropriate use, but the real gating factor is hospital economics.”

⸻

4. “Does the 48-hour infusion create a barrier?”

“It creates friction, not a barrier.

It:
	•	Increases ICU workload
	•	Requires coordination and bag changes

But:
	•	Patients are already in ICU post-EVT
	•	The infusion reinforces, not changes, site of care

It is a manageable operational tradeoff, not a disqualifier.”

⸻

5. “What is the single biggest risk?”

“Misalignment between price and reimbursement timing.

If:
	•	Price is set assuming NTAP or DRG adjustment
	•	But those are delayed or weaker than expected

Then adoption stalls, regardless of clinical performance.”

⸻

6. “What is the single biggest unlock?”

“NTAP approval with strong Phase 3 outcomes.

That combination:
	•	Reduces financial risk immediately
	•	Signals CMS validation
	•	Creates the bridge to DRG recalibration

Without that, adoption remains limited.”

⸻

Final Positioning Line (Use if needed)

“This is not a clinical adoption problem. It is a reimbursement timing and hospital economics problem.”
