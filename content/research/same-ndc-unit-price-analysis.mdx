## Purpose

This research examines whether same-NDC unit-price behavior explains observed coverage and tier differences across Medicare Part D plans. Analysts frequently aggregate at molecule level, but plan behavior can diverge even within identical NDC identifiers.

By focusing on same-NDC observations over time, the analysis reduces confounding from product mix changes and clarifies where formulary behavior appears to diverge from direct price signals.

## Data Construction

The dataset links plan-year formulary attributes with NDC-level unit pricing and coverage fields. Records are deduplicated and normalized to preserve consistent comparison windows.

Core construction steps:

1. Build a same-NDC panel by plan-year.
2. Identify records with sufficient continuity for trend analysis.
3. Assign coverage and tier attributes at each observation point.
4. Segment records by plan type and region.

This panel design supports within-NDC comparison across different plan environments.

## Why Same-NDC Matters

Aggregate molecule analysis can hide behavior that is visible only when identical NDC records are compared. Two plans can price similarly at unit level and still apply different tier and utilization frameworks. That divergence is central to access strategy.

Same-NDC analysis therefore provides a cleaner way to test whether pricing alone explains coverage differences.

## Analytical Metrics

| Metric | Description | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Within-NDC Price Variance | Dispersion in unit price for same NDC across plans | Detects heterogeneity in plan purchasing context |
| Tier Assignment Spread | Distribution of tier levels for same NDC | Shows access design divergence |
| Control Overlay Rate | Prevalence of PA/step edits on same NDC | Captures friction beyond tier assignment |
| Persistence of Differential | Stability of differences over time | Distinguishes transient noise from structural pattern |

## Findings Direction

Initial directional findings indicate repeated tier and control divergence for same-NDC observations where unit-price differences are modest. In practical terms, this means coverage behavior is often governed by broader plan strategy rather than a simple claim-level price relationship.

In some segments, same-NDC observations show relatively narrow price bands paired with wide tier assignment spread. This is one of the strongest indicators that benefit architecture and payer economics are active drivers.

## Commercial Implications

For market access planning, same-NDC divergence signals that:

- coverage expectations should be plan-specific,
- pricing assumptions alone are insufficient for friction forecasting,
- and account-level engagement should prioritize plans with recurring control intensity despite stable unit-price context.

This is particularly relevant in categories where products are clinically substitutable and plan levers can be used to shape utilization economically.

## Policy Interpretation

From a policy standpoint, same-NDC divergence complicates broad claims about affordability progress based only on aggregate price movement. If identical products experience different access conditions independent of material price differences, policy evaluation requires plan-behavior indicators.

## Limitations

The analysis does not include full contracting detail and therefore cannot fully decompose net-price pathways. It is also sensitive to data continuity thresholds when building same-NDC panels.

Even with those limits, repeated divergence across periods supports the conclusion that same-NDC pricing signals and access signals should be interpreted jointly, not separately.

## Next Steps

Planned enhancements include:

- linking same-NDC results to patient out-of-pocket proxies,

- and producing a reproducible signal score for same-NDC access divergence.

## Robustness Checks

To strengthen interpretation, robustness checks are run on sub-panels with stricter continuity requirements. Results remain directionally consistent: same-NDC observations can show substantial access divergence even when price dispersion remains modest.

Additional checks include excluding extreme outliers and re-running segment stratification. The primary signal persists, suggesting that divergence is not driven by a narrow set of anomalous observations.

## Implications for Forecast Architecture

Forecast models often assume a monotonic relationship between unit pricing and access quality. Same-NDC analysis suggests that this assumption is weak in classes where plan design levers are actively used.

A stronger forecast architecture includes separate components for:

- price movement,
- tier/control movement,
- and segment-specific execution risk.

This separation improves scenario quality and reduces overconfidence in price-only assumptions.

## Analytical Workflow for Ongoing Use

A repeatable quarterly workflow can be built from this analysis:

1. Refresh same-NDC panel.
2. Recompute tier spread and control overlay metrics.
3. Flag divergence molecules above threshold.
4. Route flagged molecules into payer-strategy review.
5. Update exposure assumptions in access and pricing models.

This workflow turns analysis into operating discipline.

## Extended Conclusion

Same-NDC analysis reinforces a broader finding across reimbursement strategy work: access outcomes are frequently shaped by plan behavior beyond direct price signals. Teams that monitor same-NDC divergence systematically gain earlier visibility into where friction is likely to rise.

## Scenario Layer for Decision Support

To make research outputs actionable, signal findings should be translated into scenario bands used by policy and commercial teams. A simple three-band structure is usually sufficient:

- **Stable band:** no material change in access risk; maintain baseline monitoring.
- **Watch band:** directional shift observed; prepare targeted strategy adjustments.
- **Intervention band:** persistent multi-signal deterioration; implement full response plan.

Scenario translation is critical because data-rich analysis does not automatically become decision-ready. Teams need explicit thresholds and action pathways.

One recurring lesson from this research stream is that methodological transparency is as important as directional findings. Decision-makers need confidence that a detected signal reflects market behavior rather than pipeline variation.

For that reason, each update cycle should include a short methods note describing any changes in data availability, normalization rules, or metric computation. Even small method changes can alter trend interpretation when signal margins are narrow.

A transparent methods cadence reduces false escalations and supports stronger cross-functional trust in the analysis.
