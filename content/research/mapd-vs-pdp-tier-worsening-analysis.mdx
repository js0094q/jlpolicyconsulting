## Objective

This analysis compares tier worsening behavior between MAPD and PDP plans to determine whether segment-level economic structures produce distinct access patterns for the same set of molecules.

The core hypothesis is that plan-segment economics influence formulary behavior beyond molecule-level pricing conditions.

## Segment Context

MAPD and PDP plans operate under different economic and competitive constraints. Those constraints can influence how aggressively plans deploy tiering and utilization controls.

A unified analysis that does not separate these segments can miss directional behavior that matters for forecasting.

## Data and Methods

The dataset combines plan-year tier assignments and control fields, segmented into MAPD and PDP groups. Molecules are selected based on continuity and representation thresholds.

Tier worsening is measured as the change in share of plans assigning less favorable tiers over time. Control overlays are measured in parallel to evaluate friction stacking.

Method steps:

1. Build segment-specific baseline tier distributions.
2. Track movement over subsequent periods.
3. Compare movement magnitudes by segment.
4. Overlay control-intensity changes.
5. Evaluate consistency across molecule classes.

## Observed Segment Differences

Directional results suggest that in selected classes MAPD plans show sharper tier worsening than PDP plans, while PDP plans may show steadier but less volatile movement. This is not universal, but it is persistent enough to influence strategy.

Where MAPD worsening is stronger, it often appears alongside increased control overlays. This combination amplifies practical friction even when formal coverage remains intact.

In PDP segments, worsening can be more gradual but still economically meaningful, especially when paired with deductible and cost-sharing architecture that affects patient behavior.

## Implications for Market Access Teams

Segment-aware planning is essential. Access assumptions built on blended Part D behavior can misprice risk.

Recommended adjustments:

- forecast access friction separately for MAPD and PDP,
- align field and payer strategy to segment-specific movement,
- and use segment-specific early-warning indicators for tier/control changes.

## Policy Implications

Policy interpretation should account for segment heterogeneity. A single narrative about Part D access change may hide materially different beneficiary experiences across plan types.

If affordability monitoring frameworks do not segment MAPD and PDP behavior, policy conclusions can be directionally incomplete.

## Limitations

The analysis is observational and limited by visibility into plan-specific contracting architecture. Segment differences are suggestive of economic mechanism but not causal proof.

Still, repeated divergence across periods is sufficient to justify segment-specific decision frameworks in commercialization and policy analytics.

## Next Research Steps

Future work should incorporate:

- beneficiary-level cost proxies by segment,
- geographic interaction effects,
- and class-level competitive event overlays.

These additions will improve both explanatory power and planning utility.

## Extended Segment Interpretation

Segment divergence should not be interpreted as a statistical curiosity. It has direct implications for commercialization planning, because product performance in Part D can be disproportionately influenced by the segment where friction accelerates first.

If MAPD cohorts show earlier worsening, organizations may need earlier intervention in those accounts. If PDP cohorts show slower but steady deterioration, the strategy may prioritize persistence management and long-horizon payer engagement.

## Operationalizing Segment Signals

A practical segment-monitoring scorecard can include:

- tier worsening rate,
- control escalation index,
- persistence across bid cycles,
- and patient-cost pathway sensitivity.

Using this scorecard, teams can classify segment behavior as stable, watchlist, or intervention-priority.

## Portfolio Planning Impact

Segment divergence also affects portfolio-level resource allocation. Molecules with higher MAPD concentration and rising control overlays may require different launch-support and access-maintenance tactics than molecules concentrated in PDP environments.

Without segment-aware allocation, teams risk underinvesting where friction is increasing fastest.

## Extended Conclusion

MAPD vs PDP differentiation is not optional in high-quality Part D analytics. It is a core requirement for accurate interpretation of tier movement and practical access risk. The evidence supports adopting segment-specific monitoring and decision pathways as standard practice.

## Scenario Layer for Decision Support

To make research outputs actionable, signal findings should be translated into scenario bands used by policy and commercial teams. A simple three-band structure is usually sufficient:

- **Stable band:** no material change in access risk; maintain baseline monitoring.
- **Watch band:** directional shift observed; prepare targeted strategy adjustments.
- **Intervention band:** persistent multi-signal deterioration; implement full response plan.

Scenario translation is critical because data-rich analysis does not automatically become decision-ready. Teams need explicit thresholds and action pathways.
