## Objective

This analysis evaluates how plan-design variables shape patient cost exposure in Medicare Part D, with emphasis on whether design effects outweigh unit-price trends in practical affordability estimates.

The question is central to both policy analysis and commercialization planning. If design effects dominate, price-only narratives are insufficient for decision making.

## Conceptual Framework

Patient cost exposure is generated by multiple interacting elements:

- deductible structure,
- tier assignment,
- coinsurance and copay mechanics,
- utilization controls,
- and channel requirements.

Price matters, but design determines how and when price translates into patient burden.

## Data and Modeling Steps

The workflow links pricing observations with plan-design and formulary variables. A design-adjusted exposure proxy is computed for comparative analysis across plan segments and time windows.

Method steps:

1. Normalize plan-design variables across periods.
2. Integrate product-level pricing signals.
3. Build plan-level exposure proxies.
4. Compare exposure movement vs unit-price movement.
5. Decompose differences by segment and class.

## Findings Direction

Directional output suggests that plan-design shifts can materially increase exposure proxies even when unit-price movement is muted. In these contexts, patient burden worsens through architecture rather than direct price escalation.

This pattern appears most clearly where tier worsening and control overlays occur together. The combination creates both financial and administrative burden at access points.

## Interpretation

The policy implication is that affordability monitoring frameworks should include design-adjusted metrics. Without them, measured progress can overstate practical patient relief.

The commercial implication is that market access forecasting should treat plan-design movement as a first-order variable, not a secondary modifier.

## Strategic Use Cases

For manufacturer teams:

- forecast access risk in policy transition periods,
- prioritize payer engagements where design-driven exposure is worsening,
- align field execution to segments with highest friction risk.

For policy teams:

- identify where policy objectives are dampened by implementation architecture,
- support targeted recommendations for design transparency and burden monitoring.

## Limits

The exposure proxy is an analytical construct and not a direct patient-level claims measure. It is most useful for directional comparison and trend detection rather than point-estimate precision.

Even so, repeated divergence between design-adjusted exposure and unit-price trend is a strong signal that architecture effects are central to affordability outcomes.

## Next Steps

Future work will add:

## Extended Decomposition

A deeper decomposition separates financial burden from administrative burden. Financial burden reflects direct cost-sharing exposure. Administrative burden captures the effort and delay associated with controls and channel requirements.

Both forms of burden influence patient behavior, and both should be included in affordability interpretation.

## Segment-Level Exposure Notes

Exposure effects can differ materially by plan segment. In some MAPD contexts, control architecture and channel policy drive notable shifts in practical burden even when nominal cost-sharing movement is moderate. In selected PDP contexts, deductible interaction can amplify burden despite limited control escalation.

These differences reinforce the need for segment-aware exposure models.

## Application in Policy and Access Workstreams

For policy workstreams, design-adjusted exposure metrics support more realistic interpretation of how benefit rules are functioning after implementation. For access workstreams, the same metrics improve targeting of payer engagement and patient-support planning.

A shared exposure framework also improves cross-functional communication by linking policy language to operational impact.

## Extended Conclusion

Patient cost exposure is fundamentally a design outcome, not only a price outcome. Any strategy that treats design as secondary will understate risk in policy transition periods. Incorporating design-adjusted exposure analytics improves both policy interpretation quality and commercial execution readiness.

## Scenario Layer for Decision Support

To make research outputs actionable, signal findings should be translated into scenario bands used by policy and commercial teams. A simple three-band structure is usually sufficient:

- **Stable band:** no material change in access risk; maintain baseline monitoring.
- **Watch band:** directional shift observed; prepare targeted strategy adjustments.
- **Intervention band:** persistent multi-signal deterioration; implement full response plan.

Scenario translation is critical because data-rich analysis does not automatically become decision-ready. Teams need explicit thresholds and action pathways.

A transparent methods cadence reduces false escalations and supports stronger cross-functional trust in the analysis.
