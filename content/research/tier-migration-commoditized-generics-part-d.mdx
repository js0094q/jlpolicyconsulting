## Research Objective

This analysis evaluates whether commoditized generic molecules in Medicare Part D experienced significant movement toward less favorable formulary tiers, and whether that movement can be explained by observed unit-price trends alone.

The motivation is practical. Access teams repeatedly observe patient friction in classes where underlying generic pricing appears stable. Policy discussions often assume those two signals should move together. The goal here is to test that assumption in a structured plan-level dataset.

## Data Inputs and Construction

The workflow combines multi-year Part D formulary files with pricing observations mapped at the NDC or molecule-level where possible. Files are normalized by plan-year and benefit segment to support longitudinal comparison.

Key data preparation steps include:

1. Harmonizing plan identifiers across years.
2. Constructing molecule-level groupings for commoditized generics.
3. Assigning tier status bands that preserve comparability over time.
4. Joining unit-price observations to plan-level coverage records.

The analysis intentionally separates MAPD and PDP segments because plan economics and competitive pressures differ across segments.

## Measurement Framework

Tier movement is measured through distributional change, not single-plan anecdotes. For each molecule, baseline and follow-up tier shares are calculated, then decomposed by plan segment.

A tier-worsening index is used to capture directional shift:

| Metric | Definition | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tier Share Shift | Change in share of plans assigning higher tiers | Positive values indicate worsening placement |
| Control Intensity Shift | Change in prior authorization/step edit prevalence | Indicates friction layered onto tier movement |
| Price Delta Band | Unit-price trend over same period | Tests whether movement aligns with pricing |

## Observed Pattern

Across selected molecules, tier worsening appears in multiple plan contexts even when measured unit-price movement is modest. The effect is not universal, but it is material in enough classes to challenge the assumption that tiering is primarily price-driven.

In several cases, tier worsening coincides with stable or near-flat unit-price paths. Where that occurs, explanations based solely on molecule-level price become weak. Alternative mechanisms, including class-level economics and control strategy, become more plausible.

A second pattern is heterogeneity by plan segment. MAPD plans in some classes show sharper concentration of movement than PDP counterparts, suggesting segment-specific optimization behavior.

## Interpretation in Payer Economics Terms

If tier movement were strictly price-led, we would expect directional alignment between worsening placement and rising unit-cost bands. The observed divergence implies a broader optimization logic.

That logic likely includes:

- class-level utilization shaping,
- management of aggregate formulary economics,
- channel strategy interactions,
- bid-cycle pressure affecting control design.

The implication is that commoditized generics can function as control variables in plan design even when molecule-level cost pressure is limited.

## Commercialization Implications

For manufacturers, these findings matter in at least three areas.

First, access forecasting should include plan-level behavior diagnostics rather than relying only on product price assumptions. Second, pull-through risk models should account for friction layering from tier and control changes. Third, lifecycle planning around mature molecules should monitor segment-specific tier dynamics early in bid cycles.

Teams that use only aggregate price trend data are likely to underestimate access deterioration risk in selected classes.

## Policy Implications

For policy stakeholders, this pattern raises a design question: should affordability policy rely on price trend proxies when access burden can shift through benefit architecture independent of price movement?

The findings suggest that monitoring frameworks should include plan behavior indicators, including tier migration and control intensity, not only average price measures.

## Limits

This analysis is descriptive and cannot alone establish causal attribution. Contracting detail, rebate flows, and plan-specific strategic context are not fully observable in public files.

However, the signal is directionally important. Repeated divergence between pricing trend and tier movement indicates that behavioral mechanisms beyond molecule-level price are active.

## Extended Pattern Diagnostics

To test whether the observed signal is concentrated in a small subset of outlier plans, the analysis applies a persistence check. Molecules are flagged only when worsening appears across multiple plan cohorts and across consecutive periods. This filter reduces the chance that one-off operational changes are interpreted as structural behavior.

A second diagnostic tests whether control escalation accompanies tier migration. When both move together, the burden pathway is typically stronger than when tier movement appears alone. In practical terms, the pairing suggests that plans are not simply changing labels; they are changing the operating conditions for access.

## Segment and Class Commentary

Segment behavior is not uniform. Certain classes show stronger tier-worsening concentration in MAPD cohorts, while others are more balanced across MAPD and PDP plans. This variation implies that one-size assumptions about Part D access movement are likely to miss meaningful risk in selected categories.

Class differences also matter. Commoditized classes with high substitutability may be more exposed to broad benefit architecture changes than classes with tighter clinical constraints. Teams should therefore avoid importing assumptions from one class to another without direct evidence.

## Scenario Translation for Commercial Teams

A practical scenario framework can be built from the signal stack:

- **Scenario 1: Stable pricing, limited migration.** Minimal access redesign needed, monitor only.
- **Scenario 2: Stable pricing, moderate migration.** Update payer strategy and field messaging.
- **Scenario 3: Stable pricing, migration plus controls.** Full access-risk response, including revised demand assumptions.

This scenario structure supports rapid decision alignment between policy, market access, and brand teams.

## Extended Conclusion

The evidence does not support a simplistic price-driven explanation for tier movement in commoditized generic classes. A more credible interpretation is that plan optimization behavior, operating through formulary and control design, is a major contributor.

For organizations making pricing and access decisions, this means policy interpretation must be coupled with plan-behavior analytics. For policy stakeholders, it means affordability monitoring frameworks should include behavior indicators, not just unit-price trends.
