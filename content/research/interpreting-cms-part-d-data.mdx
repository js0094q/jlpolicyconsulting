The Medicare Part D redesign changes the visible economics of prescription drug access.

For beneficiaries, the headline is straightforward: annual out-of-pocket exposure is capped, and catastrophic-phase cost sharing is eliminated. That is a material affordability change for patients with high drug costs.

For manufacturers, plans, and market access teams, the more practical question is different. If the beneficiary’s exposure is reduced, where does the pressure go?

The answer is that pressure does not disappear. It moves into plan liability, bid strategy, formulary design, utilization management, premium management, rebate negotiations, and sponsor monitoring.

## Executive Summary

The redesigned Part D benefit lowers beneficiary out-of-pocket exposure, but it also changes the incentives facing Part D plan sponsors. Under the 2025 benefit structure, beneficiaries move through a deductible, initial coverage phase, and catastrophic phase. Once the annual out-of-pocket threshold is reached, the beneficiary pays no further cost sharing for covered Part D drugs. At the same time, plan sponsors take on a larger share of drug costs in the catastrophic phase, while Medicare reinsurance becomes less dominant.

This creates a new access-management problem. Plans still need to manage total drug spending, but they have fewer opportunities to rely on high beneficiary cost exposure as the visible pressure point. As a result, pressure is more likely to show up through benefit design, tiering, formulary exclusions where permitted, prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits, pharmacy network design, rebate strategy, and tighter scrutiny of high-cost therapies.

For manufacturers, the implication is not that lower patient out-of-pocket exposure automatically improves access. It may improve affordability at the point of sale, but it can also increase plan incentives to manage utilization before the claim reaches the patient. The monitoring question should therefore shift from “what does the patient pay?” to “how does the plan control access now that patient liability is capped?”

## Research Question

What changes when Part D redesign reduces beneficiary out-of-pocket exposure?

The practical question is not whether the law lowers member liability, because it does. The question is where the plan-level pressure goes after the benefit changes, and what that means for formulary behavior, access management, rebate strategy, and sponsor monitoring.

This analysis focuses on five questions:

1. How does the redesigned benefit change the distribution of liability?
2. Why does lower beneficiary exposure increase the importance of plan-level management?
3. Where is pressure most likely to emerge in formulary and utilization management?
4. What should manufacturers monitor after the redesign?
5. How should market access teams distinguish improved affordability from improved access?

## Key Findings

### 1. The beneficiary affordability improvement is real, but incomplete as an access signal

The redesigned Part D benefit materially reduces beneficiary exposure for patients with high covered Part D drug spending. The annual out-of-pocket cap and elimination of beneficiary cost sharing in the catastrophic phase are important changes.

But lower patient liability does not necessarily mean easier access. The plan still has to manage benefit costs, and the redesign increases the financial relevance of plan liability. That means the access question moves upstream.

A beneficiary may face less cost sharing once a drug is covered and dispensed, but the plan may respond by managing whether, when, and under what conditions the drug is covered.

### 2. Plan liability becomes more strategically important

The redesigned benefit increases plan responsibility for drug costs, especially after beneficiaries reach the out-of-pocket cap. In the catastrophic phase, beneficiaries pay no cost sharing, while sponsors typically pay a larger share of costs than under the old structure.

This changes plan incentives. Plans have more direct exposure to high-cost utilization and less ability to rely on beneficiary coinsurance above the cap. That does not automatically mean plans will restrict access across the board. It does mean that plans have a stronger reason to examine high-cost categories, high-utilization products, products with close substitutes, and drugs where rebate strategy can materially affect net cost.

The result is a more active plan-management environment.

### 3. Pressure is likely to show up through formulary behavior

When beneficiary liability falls, formulary design becomes more important.

Plans can manage exposure through tier placement, preferred and non-preferred positioning, formulary coverage decisions where permitted, specialty-tier placement, and differential cost sharing in the initial coverage phase. Even with the annual out-of-pocket cap, tiering and coinsurance still matter before the cap is reached, and formulary placement remains a key tool for steering utilization.

For manufacturers, the risk is that patient affordability improves while formulary access becomes more contested. A lower patient out-of-pocket maximum may reduce abandonment for covered prescriptions, but it does not prevent plans from applying tighter access criteria.

### 4. Utilization management becomes a more important pressure valve

Prior authorization, step therapy, and quantity limits are likely to matter more in categories where plans face higher liability and where therapeutic alternatives exist.

The policy change does not eliminate plan tools. It changes the reason those tools matter. Under the redesigned benefit, utilization management can function as a way to control the flow of high-cost claims into plan liability. That makes monitoring utilization management at the plan, product, and therapeutic-class level critical.

For high-cost specialty products, the commercial question becomes whether the reduction in member out-of-pocket exposure is offset by more restrictive prior authorization, step sequencing, documentation requirements, or renewal criteria.

### 5. Monitoring should shift from patient cost alone to plan behavior

Patient cost exposure remains important, but it is no longer sufficient.

Manufacturers should monitor:

- formulary inclusion and exclusion,
- preferred versus non-preferred status,
- specialty-tier placement,
- prior authorization criteria,
- step therapy requirements,
- quantity limits,
- deductible exposure,
- coinsurance versus copay design,
- plan premium and deductible changes,
- PDP versus MA-PD differences,
- plan consolidation and sponsor-level policy changes.

The central analytical shift is from affordability monitoring to access-friction monitoring.

## Analysis

### The Part D redesign changes who feels the cost pressure

The redesigned Part D benefit lowers beneficiary exposure by capping annual out-of-pocket costs. In 2025, CMS describes the standard benefit as having three phases: deductible, initial coverage, and catastrophic coverage. In the initial coverage phase, beneficiaries generally pay 25% coinsurance until reaching the annual out-of-pocket threshold. In catastrophic coverage, beneficiaries pay no cost sharing.

That structure materially changes the patient experience for high-cost users. It reduces exposure to uncapped cost sharing and should improve affordability for beneficiaries who previously faced large prescription drug liabilities.

But the liability does not vanish from the system. It is redistributed.

Plans, manufacturers, and Medicare now divide responsibility differently across the benefit. In the catastrophic phase, plan sponsors typically pay 60% of covered Part D drug costs. Manufacturers generally provide discounts for applicable drugs, and Medicare pays reinsurance at a reduced share relative to the old system.

This matters because plan sponsors now have greater reason to manage the drugs that drive high-cost exposure.

### Lower beneficiary exposure may increase utilization

Lower out-of-pocket costs can reduce abandonment and improve persistence for some patients. That is a central policy goal of the redesign.

But from the plan perspective, lower beneficiary exposure may also increase utilization, especially among patients who previously delayed, rationed, or abandoned high-cost prescriptions because of cost. If more patients initiate or stay on therapy because the cost barrier is lower, total plan liability can rise.

MedPAC’s 2026 Part D analysis reported that Part D actuaries viewed the shift to greater plan liability as increasing uncertainty and risk for plans, and that utilization among enrollees most affected by the IRA cost-sharing protections may have been underestimated in 2025 bidding.

That dynamic is important for market access strategy. A product may benefit from improved affordability at the patient level, but plans may respond to the expected utilization effect through tighter management.

### Formulary strategy becomes a more direct expression of plan risk

Part D plans have several tools to manage spending. They can adjust formulary coverage, preferred status, tier placement, cost-sharing structure, pharmacy network design, and utilization management.

The redesign makes those tools more commercially important because the plan’s own liability is more directly exposed. If a plan is responsible for more of the cost once patients move through the benefit, then the plan has stronger incentives to influence which drugs are used, in what sequence, and under what conditions.

That does not mean every category becomes more restrictive. The effect will vary by class, competitive dynamics, rebate leverage, clinical substitutability, protected-class status, and CMS formulary rules. But it does mean that access strategy should assume more plan-level scrutiny, especially for products that are high-cost, high-volume, weakly differentiated, or exposed to therapeutic alternatives.

### PDP and MA-PD behavior should not be assumed to move identically

Standalone Prescription Drug Plans and Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug plans operate under different business models. MA-PD sponsors can use Medicare Advantage rebate dollars to reduce or eliminate the Part D premium, while PDPs rely more directly on the economics of the drug benefit itself.

That difference matters after redesign. A benefit change that increases plan liability may show up differently in PDP premiums, deductibles, formularies, and utilization management than in MA-PD design. MA-PD sponsors may have more flexibility to manage premiums through broader plan economics, while PDPs may face more direct pressure in bids and benefit design.

For manufacturers, this means Part D monitoring should separate PDP and MA-PD behavior. Aggregated Part D averages can conceal meaningful differences by plan type, sponsor, region, and enrollment mix.

### The commercial risk is mistaking affordability improvement for access improvement

The redesign clearly improves affordability for many beneficiaries with high covered Part D drug costs. But affordability is only one dimension of access.

A product can become less expensive for a patient at the pharmacy counter while becoming harder to access through prior authorization or step therapy. Conversely, a product may remain broadly covered, but its preferred status or tier placement may deteriorate as plans seek to manage liability.

The access question after redesign is therefore more layered:

- Is the product covered?
- Is it preferred?
- Is it subject to prior authorization?
- Are criteria more restrictive than prior years?
- Is step therapy required?
- Is cost sharing structured as coinsurance or copay?
- Does the patient face deductible exposure before the cap?
- Does the plan manage the category differently across PDP and MA-PD books?
- Does the plan’s policy differ for new starts versus continuing patients?

Those questions are more useful than asking only whether the patient’s annual out-of-pocket maximum is lower.

## Practical Implications

### For manufacturers

The redesigned benefit may improve affordability for patients who obtain covered therapy, but it should not be treated as automatic access expansion.

Manufacturers should pressure-test whether lower patient out-of-pocket exposure is being offset by tighter formulary management. This requires plan-level and sponsor-level monitoring, not just national summaries.

Priority questions include:

- Are more plans adding prior authorization?
- Are step requirements expanding?
- Are products moving to less preferred tiers?
- Are plans shifting from copays to coinsurance?
- Are products exposed to deductible design before the cap?
- Are MA-PD and PDP formularies diverging?
- Are high-cost products facing tighter renewal criteria?
- Are rebate negotiations becoming more important for preferred status?

### For market access teams

Market access strategy should move from a patient-cost-only narrative to a plan-liability narrative.

The argument should not simply be that beneficiaries pay less. The more commercially relevant argument is how the product fits within plan incentives under redesigned liability. This may require stronger evidence on appropriate utilization, persistence, adherence, comparative value, budget impact, and differentiated clinical need.

Field teams should be prepared to explain how access controls affect real patient flow after redesign. Lower OOP exposure may support adherence, but tighter plan management can still delay starts.

### For policy and government affairs teams

The redesign should be assessed through both beneficiary and market behavior outcomes.

A beneficiary-level affordability success can coexist with increased plan management. That does not necessarily mean the policy failed. It means the policy shifted the locus of control. Monitoring should therefore include access behavior, not just statutory benefit design.

Policy teams should track whether plan responses differ by:

- therapeutic class,
- protected-class status,
- high-cost specialty category,
- PDP versus MA-PD,
- sponsor,
- region,
- low-income subsidy status,
- negotiated versus non-negotiated product status.

### For analytics teams

Analytics should be built around plan behavior over time.

A useful monitoring model should compare pre- and post-redesign plan behavior across the same product, class, sponsor, and plan type. The unit of analysis should not only be product-level average cost sharing. It should include plan-by-product formulary status, tier placement, utilization management flags, estimated beneficiary out-of-pocket exposure, and competitive access position.

## Methodology and Source Notes

This article synthesizes CMS Part D redesign guidance, Medicare Part D benefit descriptions, and MedPAC analysis of plan bid and liability changes following implementation of the Inflation Reduction Act benefit redesign.

The analysis is interpretive. It does not claim that every plan will respond identically or that utilization management will uniformly increase across all classes. Instead, it identifies the incentive channels most likely to matter when beneficiary out-of-pocket liability is reduced and plan liability becomes more prominent.

Any empirical extension of this analysis should rely on CMS Part D formulary, cost-sharing, plan information, and public use files, with results stratified by plan type, sponsor, region, therapeutic class, and enrollment weighting where available.

## Conclusion

Part D redesign lowers beneficiary out-of-pocket exposure, but it does not remove cost pressure from the system. It reallocates that pressure.

For beneficiaries with high drug costs, the redesigned benefit can materially improve affordability. For plans, the same redesign increases the importance of managing liability through bids, premiums, formulary structure, utilization management, rebate strategy, and plan design. For manufacturers, the key access question becomes more complex: lower patient cost exposure may improve affordability after coverage is secured, but plans may respond by tightening the pathway to coverage.

The practical conclusion is that post-redesign Part D monitoring should not stop at beneficiary out-of-pocket cost. It should track where pressure reappears: formulary placement, utilization management, plan-type differences, sponsor behavior, and category-specific access controls.

The central market access question after redesign is not whether patients are better protected from high out-of-pocket costs. They are. The question is how plans manage the liability that remains.
