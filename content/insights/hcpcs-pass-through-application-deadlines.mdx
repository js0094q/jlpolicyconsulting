For provider-administered drugs, timing is part of reimbursement design.

HCPCS Level II coding and OPPS transitional pass-through are separate decisions, but they often shape the same launch problem: whether a product has a clean billing path when providers first need to use it.

## Executive summary

CMS treats HCPCS drug and biological applications on a quarterly cadence. For 2026, the deadlines are January 2, April 1, July 1, and October 1. Separate OPPS pass-through applications are accepted on an ongoing basis, but CMS still expects them early enough to fit the quarter in which pass-through status is sought.

CMS also requires pass-through applications for drugs, biologicals, and radiopharmaceuticals to be submitted through MEARIS.

The commercial point is simple: a product that is hard to code or hard to pay is harder to operationalize. That matters for launch planning, provider education, revenue cycle readiness, and hospital adoption.

## The core distinction

HCPCS and pass-through are related, but they do not solve the same problem.

HCPCS helps define the product's claims identity. A permanent, product-specific code reduces ambiguity and makes billing cleaner for hospitals and provider offices.

Pass-through is a payment status question. Under OPPS, qualifying drugs, biologicals, and radiopharmaceuticals can receive separate temporary payment while CMS gathers claims and cost data.

In practice, missing the HCPCS window can delay coding clarity. Missing the pass-through window can delay better outpatient payment treatment. Missing both can leave the launch more dependent on miscellaneous billing or temporary workarounds than it should be.

## HCPCS and OPPS pass-through timing

| Process | CMS submission timing | Earliest practical effective date | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HCPCS Level II, drugs and biologicals, first-quarter filing | First business day of January | July 1 | A permanent product-specific code reduces billing ambiguity and can make hospitals and providers more willing to operationalize the product |
| HCPCS Level II, drugs and biologicals, second-quarter filing | First business day of April | October 1 | Missing the earlier quarter can push clean billing implementation deeper into launch |
| HCPCS Level II, drugs and biologicals, third-quarter filing | First business day of July | January 1 | Can position the product for a cleaner start to the next calendar year |
| HCPCS Level II, drugs and biologicals, fourth-quarter filing | First business day of October | April 1 | Missing this can leave a product in a messier billing posture for longer |
| OPPS transitional pass-through, first-quarter window | First business day in March | July 1 | Creates the earliest mid-year opportunity for separate pass-through treatment if approved |
| OPPS transitional pass-through, second-quarter window | First business day in June | October 1 | Important for products launching into the second half of the year |
| OPPS transitional pass-through, third-quarter window | First business day in September | January 1 | Helps align outpatient payment visibility with the new calendar year |
| OPPS transitional pass-through, fourth-quarter window | First business day in December | April 1 | Missing this can defer better outpatient economics into a later quarter |

Note: CMS states that HCPCS drug and biological applications are due on the first business day of each quarter. CMS quarterly coding determinations show that first-quarter filings can take effect July 1, second-quarter filings October 1, third-quarter filings January 1, and fourth-quarter filings April 1. Pass-through timing uses a separate OPPS application timetable, and CMS notes that longer review can be required if an application is incomplete or additional information is needed.

## Why the deadlines matter

These deadlines are not just administrative.

A permanent HCPCS code helps providers and hospitals build a recognizable claims path. That reduces internal confusion, makes education easier, and lowers the chance that teams fall back on temporary or miscellaneous billing behavior while they wait for the product to settle into routine use.

Pass-through matters for a different reason. CMS's CY 2026 hospital outpatient materials state that pass-through drugs, biologicals, and radiopharmaceuticals are paid at ASP + 6 percent, or the biosimilar equivalent benchmark CMS applies in that setting. That can materially improve outpatient payment visibility at launch.

The distinction becomes especially important in 340B hospitals. CMS's 340B billing guidance explains that separately payable, non-pass-through 340B-acquired drugs are billed with the appropriate 340B modifier, while pass-through drugs are treated differently under OPPS payment policy. That means pass-through status can change the economics of early uptake for covered entities that depend on hospital outpatient reimbursement.

The operational implication is direct. If coding is not settled, billing teams have more friction. If pass-through is also missed, the outpatient economics may be less favorable than they could have been. In hospital outpatient settings, that combination can slow uptake even when clinical interest is strong.

## Conclusion

For provider-administered products, HCPCS and OPPS pass-through deadlines should be treated as launch deadlines.

A clean HCPCS code can reduce provider and hospital friction. Pass-through can improve outpatient payment visibility and hospital economics. Teams that miss these windows may still launch, but they often do so with more operational resistance than necessary.
