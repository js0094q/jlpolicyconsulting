The Part D redesign should be described plainly: it improves beneficiary financial protection. CMS guidance for 2026 continues the redesigned Part D framework, and Medicare.gov explains that Part D out-of-pocket spending for covered drugs reaches a $2,100 threshold in 2026 before catastrophic coverage begins. [1][2][5]

But lower annual exposure does not automatically make a medicine easy to obtain. Patients can still encounter deductible exposure, tiering, prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits, pharmacy routing, and exception processes before they benefit from the cap.

## Key Takeaways

- The redesigned Part D benefit improves annual financial protection for beneficiaries. [1][2][5][7]
- Patients may still face cost shock at the pharmacy because plan costs can include deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance before the annual threshold is reached. [5]
- Prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits, and exceptions can still delay therapy or shift work to patients, caregivers, pharmacists, and prescribers. [4][6]
- Access assessment should ask whether a patient can complete the path to treatment, not only whether statutory liability changed.

## What changed for patients

The most visible patient benefit is financial protection. KFF describes Part D changes under the Inflation Reduction Act, including the out-of-pocket drug spending cap that is set at $2,100 in 2026. [7] Medicare.gov explains that once a beneficiary reaches the 2026 threshold for covered Part D drugs, catastrophic coverage begins and the beneficiary has no further out-of-pocket costs for covered Part D drugs for the rest of the calendar year. [5]

That protection matters most for patients with high covered drug costs. It can reduce the risk that annual exposure becomes open-ended.

The practical access question, however, starts earlier. A patient still has to get the prescription filled, approved, routed, and supported. If the first claim produces deductible exposure, coinsurance, a prior authorization edit, or a step-therapy requirement, the patient may still wait before treatment begins.

## Where barriers remain

| Remaining barrier | Patient encounter | Why the cap does not solve it alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deductible or coinsurance | The patient pays more than expected at the pharmacy counter. | The cap limits annual exposure, but the first-fill amount may still be difficult. [5] |
| Higher tier | The covered drug has less favorable cost sharing. | The patient may ask for a substitute or seek a tiering exception. [6] |
| Prior authorization | The plan requires approval before covering the drug. | The patient may wait while the prescriber submits medical information. [4] |
| Step therapy | The plan requires a less expensive therapy first unless an exception is approved. | The patient may switch therapy or wait for exception review. [4][6] |
| Pharmacy network | The patient must use a specific channel or preferred pharmacy. | Travel, caregiver coordination, and refill timing can still disrupt access. |

External claims research in direct oral anticoagulants found that patients with abandoned first prescriptions had higher out-of-pocket costs than patients with approved claims. [8] That evidence should not be generalized mechanically to every Part D product, but it supports the practical point that counter cost remains an access risk even in a benefit with stronger annual protection.

## What should not be overclaimed

The redesign does not prove that every sponsor will tighten access. It does not prove patient harm from any single formulary field. It does not make every access problem a redesign problem.

The stronger claim is more precise. The redesign improves the financial architecture, while plan design still determines many steps in the patient pathway. CMS public files allow tiering, utilization management, beneficiary cost, pharmacy network, and pricing fields to be monitored. [3]

That monitoring should be patient-facing. Does the barrier create cost shock, delay to therapy, prescription abandonment risk, therapy switching pressure, continuity disruption, or caregiver and office burden?

## What teams should do

Manufacturers should update access dashboards to distinguish annual financial protection from first-fill usability. A patient may benefit from the cap and still need affordability support, prior authorization help, or exception support.

Payers and PBMs should evaluate whether plan rules are clear, clinically justified, and operationally navigable. If the patient or prescriber cannot understand the pathway, coverage may exist but access still fails.

Policy teams should communicate the redesign as an important protection without implying that all access barriers have been removed. The patient experience depends on both benefit law and plan operations.

## Conclusion

Part D redesign is a major beneficiary-protection reform. It is not the end of access monitoring.

The patient-centered standard is whether the medicine can be afforded, approved, filled, and continued through the actual plan pathway. Lower annual exposure helps, but it does not automatically remove the steps patients must clear before therapy begins.
