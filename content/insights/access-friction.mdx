Coverage looks clean on a slide. Access usually does not.

That gap matters.

A product can be on formulary and still be difficult to obtain, prescribe, or stay on. The reason is simple: access friction is layered. It sits in tier placement, utilization management, channel requirements, deductible exposure, and pharmacy preference design, not just in the yes-or-no question of whether a product is covered. CMS Part D public files are useful because they let you examine those layers separately.

That is why covered lives can be a comfortingly incomplete metric.

## The Four Layers of Practical Access

### 1. Formulary Position

Tiering affects what patients pay and how a product is framed relative to alternatives.

### 2. Utilization Management

Prior authorization, quantity limits, and step therapy often determine how much work stands between prescribing and filling.

### 3. Channel Design

Preferred retail, standard retail, preferred mail, and non-preferred mail each change the path to therapy. Sometimes the friction is not no. It is yes, but only this way.

### 4. Patient Cost Pathway

Coinsurance, copays, deductible application, and phase-specific benefit design shape the actual experience. This is where policy, formulary design, and economics meet operational reality.

## Why This Matters Commercially

Field teams experience friction before senior leadership sees it in a dashboard. Pull-through weakens, prescribing gets more conditional, and product performance can drift for reasons that are technically visible but operationally underestimated.

That is why access strategy should measure friction, not just coverage.

## Better Question, Better Answer

The wrong question is:

> Is the product covered?

The better question is:

> How many things have to go right before the patient actually gets it?

## Bottom Line

Access friction is where payer behavior becomes visible.

If you want to understand reimbursement reality, start there.
