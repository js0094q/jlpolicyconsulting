The redesigned Part D benefit improves financial protection for beneficiaries. It does not guarantee that a patient can leave the pharmacy with the medicine in hand.

Coverage answers whether the plan lists the drug. Access answers whether the patient can afford it, fill it at the intended pharmacy, satisfy any plan rules, and start treatment without avoidable delay. CMS public files make many of those access mechanics visible, including tier level, prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits, beneficiary cost information, pharmacy network data, and drug pricing fields. [3]

## Key Takeaways

- A covered Part D drug can still be hard for a patient to start if the claim produces high cost sharing, prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits, or pharmacy routing problems. [3][4][5]
- Prior authorization and step therapy turn access into a waiting process. The patient may leave the pharmacy while the prescriber, plan, and support team exchange documentation. [4][6]
- The 2026 out-of-pocket cap improves annual protection, but deductible exposure, coinsurance, tier placement, and pharmacy network design still shape the first-fill experience. [1][2][5][7]
- Access monitoring should ask whether a real patient can complete the pathway, not only whether the drug is listed.

## Where the patient encounters friction

Most access friction becomes visible late in the process. A patient may have a prescription, believe the medicine is covered, and only learn the practical condition when the pharmacy submits the claim.

The result can be a higher counter price than expected, a message that prior authorization is required, a step-therapy edit, a quantity-limit issue, or a pharmacy-network problem. Medicare.gov describes prior authorization as approval that the patient or prescriber must get before the plan will cover certain drugs, and step therapy as a requirement to try a less expensive formulary drug before moving to a more expensive one. [4]

For the patient, those are not abstract plan controls. They can mean another call to the prescriber, a wait for supporting documentation, an exception request, a different pharmacy, or a decision to postpone the medicine.

## Why coverage can still fail the patient

Coverage can fail in practice without a formal denial. The drug may be listed, but the listed pathway may be too expensive, slow, or confusing to complete.

| Mechanism | Patient encounter | Possible consequence | What reduces burden |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Higher tier or coinsurance | The pharmacy returns a higher out-of-pocket amount than the patient expected. | The patient may delay the fill, ask for a cheaper alternative, or abandon the prescription. | Pre-claim affordability checks, clear benefit messaging, and early support triage. |
| Prior authorization | The claim pauses until the plan receives clinical information. | The patient may leave without the medicine and wait while the office submits documentation. | Office-ready criteria, faster documentation workflows, and proactive hub follow-up. |
| Step therapy | The plan requires another product first unless an exception is approved. | The patient may switch therapy or wait for an exception if the prescriber believes the requested drug is needed. | Exception support, prescriber education, and clear alternative-pathway guidance. |
| Pharmacy routing | The patient is told to use a different network or preferred pharmacy. | The fill may be delayed, especially for patients with transportation, caregiver, or digital-access barriers. | Pharmacy-channel mapping and patient-facing routing support. |

Higher out-of-pocket exposure deserves particular attention. Medicare.gov explains that Part D costs can include deductible exposure, copayments, and coinsurance, and that the 2026 deductible can be as high as $615 before the plan starts to pay. [5] External claims research in direct oral anticoagulants found higher out-of-pocket costs among abandoned first prescriptions than approved claims, supporting the practical concern that high counter costs can become a fill-completion risk. [8]

## What teams should monitor

A useful access view should start with the patient path.

First, can the patient fill at the expected pharmacy? Preferred network and pharmacy data matter because a covered drug can still become inconvenient if the patient has to reroute.

Second, can the patient afford the first fill? The annual cap matters for high-cost exposure, but the first claim can still involve deductible, coinsurance, or tier-driven cost sharing. [5][7]

Third, can the prescriber and office satisfy plan rules quickly? CMS describes tiering and formulary exceptions as processes that require prescriber support when the patient seeks lower cost sharing, waiver of a utilization management requirement, or access to a non-formulary drug. [6]

Fourth, can vulnerable patients complete the process? Older adults, disabled beneficiaries, patients with multiple chronic conditions, and lower-income patients can be more exposed to paperwork, transportation limits, caregiver dependence, and upfront cost pressure.

## Why it matters

Manufacturers should not treat covered lives as a patient-access metric by itself. Covered lives can overstate practical reach when the patient pathway includes cost shock, administrative waiting, or channel rerouting.

Payers and PBMs should evaluate whether management tools create avoidable patient burden. Prior authorization, step therapy, and quantity limits can be legitimate plan-design tools, but they should be operationally clear and clinically workable.

Policy teams should separate two conclusions. Part D redesign improves financial protection. It does not eliminate the need to monitor whether patients can start and continue therapy through the actual plan pathway.

## Conclusion

Coverage is the beginning of the access question, not the end.

The patient-centered test is direct: when the claim is processed, can the patient afford the medicine, satisfy the plan rules, use the pharmacy channel, and start therapy without avoidable delay? If the answer is uncertain, the product is covered but access is still at risk.
