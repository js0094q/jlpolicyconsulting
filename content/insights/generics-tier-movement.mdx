There is a persistent assumption in pharmaceutical policy and market access:  
**lower price should translate to better access.**

The CMS Part D data do not consistently support that.

## What the Data Show

Analysis of 2025Q4 versus 2026Q1 formulary files identifies a subset of mature generics with **meaningful net movement into higher tiers despite minimal same-NDC unit price change**.

Representative examples include:

| Product | Net Higher-Tier Movement | Same-NDC Unit Price Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nortriptyline HCl | +23.6 pp | +0.7% |
| Alprazolam | +15.2 pp | +1.6% |
| Morphine Sulfate | +7.0 pp | +3.5% |
| Dexmethylphenidate HCl | +6.8 pp | -0.1% |
| Oxycodone HCl | +6.4 pp | +1.5% |

Source: CMS Part D SPUF-derived analysis comparing plan-level tier placement and same-NDC pricing across quarters.

The pattern is consistent across multiple high-volume, mature generic categories.

## What This Means (Observed, Not Assumed)

The data support a narrow but important conclusion:

> **Tier placement is not determined by unit price alone.**

This is not a theoretical argument. It is a direct observation from plan-level formulary behavior.

## What the Data Do Not Prove

The data do **not** show intent. They do not prove that plans are targeting specific drugs or pursuing a single unified strategy.

They show something more useful:

- tier worsening can occur without meaningful price pressure,
- movement can repeat across products and plans,
- access design is influenced by factors beyond unit cost.

## Likely Mechanisms (Inference)

Given how Part D plans operate, several mechanisms are consistent with this pattern:

- class-level formulary normalization,
- utilization management alignment,
- channel or dispensing strategy adjustments,
- bid optimization across high-volume categories.

These are inference-based explanations, grounded in how plans manage formularies, not directly observable from the files themselves.

## Why This Matters Commercially

If access teams assume pricing is the primary driver of access, they will miss this pattern.

A product can:

- maintain stable unit pricing,
- remain widely covered,
- and still experience increasing access friction through tier and control design.

That has direct implications for:

- pull-through,
- prescriber behavior,
- patient persistence.

## How to Operationalize This Signal

This pattern becomes actionable when tracked systematically.

Three linked views are sufficient:

1. **Tier Movement Tracker**  
   Identify recurring higher-tier movement within mature generic classes.
2. **Control Overlay Tracker**  
   Monitor prior authorization, step therapy, and quantity limits alongside tier changes.
3. **Price vs Access Divergence**  
   Flag cases where price remains stable but access conditions worsen.

## Bottom Line

The relationship between price and access is conditional, not automatic.

The data show that **incentive structure, not unit price, is often the binding constraint on access design.**
